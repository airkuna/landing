Your €10,000 in the bank:
you get zero, the banks break records

Over €30 billion of household savings sits in current accounts at almost 0% interest. At the same time, banks in Croatia are breaking profit records. Where does that return go — and what can you do with your own money?

24 July 20265 min readSources: CNB · ECB · CBS

Open your banking app and look at the balance. For most people in Croatia there are a few thousand euros sitting in a current or giro account — money kept just in case, for security. It feels good: the money is "safe". But while it sits there, that money does absolutely nothing for you — and does very well for somebody else.

ProblemYour money is asleep. Literally.

The average resident of Croatia holds about €10,700 in a bank. The bulk of household savings — nearly €32 billion — sits in current and giro (overnight) accounts. Interest on those accounts is effectively zero: on the order of 0.2%, and even less on pure transaction accounts.1

In other words: your €10,000, after a year in a current account, earns you — a few euros. With inflation, in real terms it loses value every month.

ProblemBut that money is working somewhere — for the bank

The bank does not keep your deposit in a vault. It lends out most of it, and can simply park excess liquidity at the central bank. That is where the catch is: while the European Central Bank (ECB) paid banks up to 4.00% a year on that parked excess (from September 2023 to June 2024),2 the banks paid you about 0.2% on the very same money in your account.3

The bank keeps the difference of almost four percentage points. What does that amount to? In 2024 alone the Croatian National Bank paid banks over €530 million in interest on overnight deposits — money earned by parking excess liquidity, without a single loan being approved.4

"You get ~0%. The bank gets the return. Legal and normal — but it is your money doing the work, while someone else takes the reward."

ProblemThe scale: this is not pocket change

Multiply your few thousand euros by everyone in Croatia and you get numbers that change your perspective:

To put that €1.53 billion in context: it is roughly 75% of the entire Croatian general government deficit for 2024 (about €2.0bn).6 The banks alone, then, earned the equivalent of three quarters of the state's annual shortfall. And since around 90% of the banking sector is foreign-owned, most of that profit leaves the country as dividends.5

None of this is a conspiracy theory — these are publicly published rates and figures from the ECB, the Croatian National Bank and the Croatian Bureau of Statistics. The system works exactly as designed. The only question is: why should you be its source rather than its beneficiary?
